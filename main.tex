\chapter{Goldfish Don't Bark}
'The nice thing about goldfish is that they don't bark!' said Granny, as she stood back from the goldfish tank to admire the pretty gold, orange, yellow and striped aquarium fish as they glided about their silent, glassed-in enclosure.
\par
'They don't make any noise at all,' said her granddaughter Koki, who had joined her in front of the tank. 'They don't bark like dogs, they don't bray like donkeys, they don't sing like birds. How do they talk to each other?'
\par
'They can communicate without talking' said Granny. 'Do you ever see them bump into each other? Humans do a lot of talking, and ever they keep bumping into each other.'
\par
Granny had acquired the aquarium recently. For over a month, she kept a dog, a scruffy-looking terrier, but the dog would bark all day and all night. In barked other dogs, it barked at squirrels, it barked at mice (but never chased them), it barked at shadows, it barked with doors creaked, it barked at autumn leaves falling from the chestnut trees.
\par
Granny couldn't stand so much barking. So, she gave the dog to her sister, who lived on the other side of the hill. Her sister liked dogs that barked. Some people do. Granny preferred goldfish.
\par
What did Granny look like? Well she looked like everyone's nice Granny-kind, while haired, a little plump, wearing an old pair of spectacles. And she smelled nice, like freshly baked bread.
\par
And what did Koki like?
\par
She looked like everyone's sweetest, prettiest little sister. What more can I say?
\par
She had two homes-one in the city, with her parents, and another here in foothills with her grandmother, whose like cottage was in a forest of Himalayan oak and maple trees. Koki spent as much time as she could with her grandmother, over the summer holidays, the winter break, even on Holi or Dussehra. In India, children gets lots of homework, but they also get a lot of holidays in which to do it!
Koki was doing her homework when she noticed a large ginger-coloured cat sitting at an open window, staring a gold fish at the other end of the room. On warm summer days, the window was usually kept wide open. It opened on to the trees, and sometimes a bird from the forest a-wagtail or a bulbul or a scarlet minivet-would hop in, explore the room and then return to the forest.
\par
Granny didn't mind these visitors-in fact, she welcomed them-and she didn't mind the cat, either, at least it didn't bark!
\par
But Granny had forgotten that cats like fish. Not because they are pretty, but because, for a cat, even a small fish is a tasty dish.
\par
Koki was noticed the cat. She stroked and petted it and the cat went away. Koki closed the window.
\par
But the next day, the cat was back, and this time it managed to conceal itself behind the window curtains. Koki had gone into the kitchen to help Granny make lunch. Suddenly, they heard a crash in the front room, and a strangled cry from the cat. Granny and Koki dropped what they were doing and ran to see what had happened.
\par
They found the cat upside down in the goldfish tank, half in and half out of the water. It had climbed up the stand, but had been unable to balance itself on the edge of the tank and fallen in. The tank was half empty and two goldfish lay on the carpet, flopping about helplessly.
\par
Koki got the cat out of the tank with some difficultly. It showed no gratitude and fled from the house, growling in shock and anger. It wouldn't be back for some time!
\par
But Granny had lost two goldfish, and now only three remained in the tank. So the next day, when they visited the Mussoorie Mall, they bought three new goldfish. One was a very pretty, deep pink little fellow, who was to become Koki's favourite.
\vspace{0.5in}
\hline
\vspace{0.5in}
Summer sped by and pre-monsoon showers swept across the hills. The grass on the hillside was transformed from a dull brown to a lush green. Tiny streams became torrents fo rushing water. The insect world came to life. Cicadas sang in the oak trees. A slender praying mantis balanced on the windowsill. At night, months flew into the front room, and sometimes the months flew into the front room, and sometimes the months flew into the goldfish tank and drowned.
\par
Beetles kept blundering in through the open window. They seemed to be attracted by the bulb above the goldfish bowl. Every now in through the open window. They seemed to be attracted by the bulb above the goldfish bowl. Every now and then, one of them would fall into the water, and Koki would have to fish it out. First, an elegant bamboo beetle. Then, next evening, a clumsy bamboo beetle, followed by a stag, floating, struggling, sinking, until Koki came to rescue.
\par
Naturally, the gold fish were alarmed. FLOP! SPLASH! Every now and then, one of those clumsy beetles would land in the tank and be unable to get out. The goldfish were used to stillness, silence and calm water. The beetles were a terrible nuisance, and finally, Koki had to keep the window closed towards evening, when the lights came on.
\par
The goldfish looked grateful.
\par
Koki put her face against the glass of the tank, and her favourite pink goldfish came up to the glass and for a time, they were nose to nose. It might have been trying to say a something. Who knows?
\par
Then came a dog that didn't bark.
\par
Determined that Granny should keep a dog, her sister had sent her a strange-looking hound, long and thin are very hairy, with a note saying it didn't bark but that it would keep the jackals away.
\par
The dog stayed with them for three days without barking. When it spotted squirrels, it whined a little and tried to chase them. And when it saw the cat, it growled and tried to look menaching. But it didn't bark-not even at the postman.
\par
Then, early one right, as the full moon came over the mountain, the dog raised his head and let out the most dreadful howl.
\par
And this set off all the dogs, jackals, wolves and foxes in the forest, and soon they were all howling: some howled at the moon, some howled at each other and some howled because it seemed like the right thing to do.
\par
This went on for about ten minutes and then it stopped just as they suddenly as it started. The moon disappeared behind a cloud and fell silent. All the animals fell asleep. The goldfish didn't seen to mind at all.
\par
Granny, being kind-hearted, didn't send the dog away.
\par
'If it's only the moon that brothers him,' she said, 'we'll just keep the curtains drawn whenever the moon comes up!'
\par
It was that simple.
\par
And the dog would be a good compainon to Granny in the days and months to come.
\par
It was time for Koki to return to her home in the city.
\par
She couldn't take the goldfish with her-not even her little pink favourite one. But she knew she would be back before long, prehaps during the winter break.
\par
She say goodbye to the dog, she said goodbye to the cat, she said goodbye to the birds in the oak trees and she gave Granny for a long, lingering, loving hug.
\par
Then she put her face to the glass of the goldfish tank and said goodbye to all her pretty, silent friend. They didn't say anything. Goldfish don't talk. But the little pink goldfish, Koki's favourite, glided up to the edge of the tank and pressed its face against the glass, right up against Koki's gentle face. Only the glass separated them. Was it trying to say something? Was it saying goodbye? Who knows?
\chapter{Boy Scouts Forever!}
I was a Boy Scout once, although I couldn't tell a slip knot form a granny knot, or a reef knot from a thief knot, expect that a thief, should you happen to catch one. I have never caught a thief and wouldn't know what to do with one since I can't tie a knot. Just let him go with a warning, I suppose. Tell him to become a Boy Scout.
\par
'Be prepared!' That's the Boy Scout motto. And a good one, too. But I never seen to be well prepared for anything, be in an exam or a journey or the roof blowing off my room. I get halfway through a speech and then forget what I have to say next. Or I make a new suit to attend a friend's and then turn up in my pyjamas.
\par
So how did I, the most impractical of boys, become a Boy Scout? I was at boarding school in Simla when it happened.
\par
Well, it seems a rumour had gone around the junior school (I was still a junior then) that I was a good cook. I had never cooked anything in my life, but I had spent a lot of time in the tuck shop making suggestions and advising samosas, jalebis, tikkis, and parkoras. For my unwanted advice, he would favour me with an occasional free samosa, so naturally I looked upon him as friend and benefactor. With this qualification, I was given a cookery badge and put in charge of our troop's supply of rations.
\par
There were about twenty of us in our troop, and during the summer break, our Scoutmaster, Mr Oliver, took us on a camping expedition to Tara Devi, a temple-crowned mountain a few miles outside Simla. That first night we were put to work, peeling potatoes, skinning onions, shelling peas and pounding masalas. These various ingredients being ready, I was asked-as the troop's cookery expert-what should be done with them. 'Put everything in the big \textit{degchi},' I ordered. 'Pour half a tin of ghee over the lot. Add some nettle leaves and cook for half and hour.'
\par
When this was done, everyone had a taste, but the general opinion was that the dish lacked something.
\par
'More salt,' I suggested.
\par
More salt was added. It still still lacked something. 'Add cup of a sugar,' I ordered.
\par
Sugar was added to the concoction. But still it lacked something.
\par
'We forgot to add tomatoes,' said Bimal, one of the Scouts.
\par
'Never mind,' I said. 'We have tomato sauce. And a bottle of tomato sauce!'
\par
'How about some vinegar?' asked another boy.
\par
'Just the thing,' I agreed.
\par
A cup of vinegar!'
\par
'Now it's too sour,' said one of the tasters.
\par
'What jam did we bring?' I asked.
\par
'Gooseberry jam.'
\par
'Just the thing. Empty the bottle!'
\par
The dish was a great success. Everyone enjoyed it, including Mr Oliver, who had no idea what went into it.
\par
'What's this called? he asked.
\par
'It's an all-Indian sweet-and-sour jam-potato curry,' I ventured.
\par
'For short, just call if a Bond-bhujji,' said Bimal. I had earned my cookery badge!
\vspace{0.5in}
\hline
\vspace{0.5in}
Poor Mr Oliver! He wasn't really cut out to be a Scoutmaster, any more than I was meant to be a Scout. The following day, he announced that he would give us a lesson in tracking. He would take a ha;f-hour start and walk into the forest, leaving behind him a trail of broken twigs, chicken feathers, pine cones and chestnuts, and we were to follow the trail until we found him.
\par
Unfortunately, we were not very good trackers. We did follow Mr Oliver's trail some way into the forest, but were distracted by a pool of clear water which looked very inviting. Abandoning our uniforms, we jumped into the pool and had a great time romping around or just lying on the grassy banks and enjoying the sunshine. A couple of hours later, feeling hungry, we returned to our campsite and set about preparing the evening meal. Bond-bhujji again, but with further variations.
\par
It was growing dark, and we were beginning to worry about Mr Oliver's whereabouts when he limped into camp, assisted by a couple of local villagers. Having waited for us at the far end of the forest for a couple of hours, he had decided to return by following his own trail, but in the gathering gloom he was soon lost. Some locals returning from the temple took charge of him and escorted him back to camp. He was very angry and made us return all our good-conduct and other badges, which he stuffed into his haversack. I had to give up my cookery badge, too.
\par
An hour later, when we were all preparing to get into our sleeping bags for the night, Mr Oliver called out, 'Where's dinner?'
\par
'We've had ours,' said Bimal. 'Everything is finished, sir.'
\par
'Where's Bond? He's supposed to be the cook. Bond, get up and make me an omelette.'
\par
'Can't, sir.'
\par
'Why not?'
\par
'You have my badge. Not allowed to cook without it. Scout rule, sir.'
\par
'Never heard such an omelette before,' confessed Mr Oliver, blowing out his cheeks.
\par
'A little too hot, but otherwise quite interesting.'
\par
'Would you like another, sir?'
\par
'Tomorrow, Bond, tomorrow. We'll breakfast early tomorrow.'
\par
But we had to break up our camp very early the next day. In the early hours, a bear had strayed into our camp, entered the tent where our stores were kept and wreaked havoc with all our provisions, even rolling our biggest degchi down the hillside.
\par
In the confusion and uproar that followed, the bear entered Mr Oliver's tent (he was already outside, fortunately) and came out entangled in Mr Oliver's dressing gown. It then made off in the direction of the forest.
\par
A bear in a dressing gown? It was a comical sight. And through we were a troop of brave little Scouts, we thought it better to let the bear keep the gown.
\chapter{The Black Cat}
Before the cat came, of course there had to be a broomstick.
\par
In the bazaar of one of our hill stations is an old junk shop-dirty, dingy and dark-in which I often potter about looking for old books or Victorian bric-a-brac. Sometimes, one comes across useful household items, but I do not usually notice these. I was, however, attracted to an old but well-preserved broom standing in a corner of a shop A long-handled broom was the rooms of my cottage, and I did not enjoy bending over double when using the common short-handled \textit{jharoo}.
\par
The old broom was priced at ten rupees. I haggled with the shopkeeper and got it for five.
\par
It was a strong broom, full of character, and I used it to good effect almost every morning. And there this story might have ended-or would never have begun-if I had not found the large black cat sitting on the garden wall.
\par
The black cat had bright yellow eyes, and it gave me a long penetrating look, as through it were summing up my possibilities as an exploitable human. Through it miaowed once or twice, I paid it no attention. I did not care much for cats. But when I went indoors, I found that the cat had followed me and begun scratching at the pantry door.
\par
It must be hungry, I thought, and gave it some milk. The cat lapped up the milk, purring deeply all the while, then sprang up on a cupboard and made itself comfortable.
\par
Well, for several days, there was no getting rid of the cat. It seemed completely at home, and merely tolerated my presence in the house. It was more interested in my broom than me, and would dance and skitter around the broom whenever I was sweeping the rooms. And when the broom was resting against the wall, the cat would slide up to it, rubbing itself against the handle and purring loudly.
\par
A cat and a broomstick-the combination was suggestive, full of possibilities . . . The cottage was old, almost a hundred years old, and I wondered about the kind of tenants it might have had during these long years. I had been in the cottage for only a year. And through it stood alone in the midst of a forest of Himalayan oaks, I had never encountered any ghosts or spirits.
\par
Miss Bellows came to see me in the middle of July. I heard the tapping of a walking stick on the rocky path outside the cottage, a tapping that stopped near the gate.
\par
'Mr Bond!' called an imperious voice. 'Are you home?'
\par
I had to been doing some gardening; I looked up to find an elderly straight-backed Englishwoman peering at me over the gate.
\par
'Good evening' I said, dropping my hoe.
\par
'I believe you have my cat,' said Miss Bellows.
\par
Through I had not met the lady before, I knew her by name and reputation. She was the oldest resident of the hill station.
'I do have a cat,' I said, 'through it's probably more correct to say that the cat has me. If she's your cat, you're welcome to her. Why don't you come in while I looked for her?'
\par
Mr Bellows stepped in. She wore a rather old-fashioned black dress, and her ancient but strong walnut stick had two or three curves in it and a knob instead of a handle.
\par
She made herself comfortable in an armchair while I went in search of the cat. But the cat was on one of her mysterious absences, and through I called for her in my most persuasive manner, she did not respond. I knew she was probably quite near. But cats are like that-preserve obstinate creatures.
\par
When the finally I returned to the sitting room, there was the cat, curled up on Miss Bellows' lap.
\par
'Well, you've got her, I see. Would you like some tea before you go?'
\par
'No, thank you,' said Miss Bellows. 'I don't drink tea.'
\par
'Something stronger, perhaps. A little brandy?' She looked up at me rather sharply. Disconcerted, I hastened to add, 'Not that I drink much, you know. I keep a little in the house for emergencies. It helps ward off colds and things. It's particularly good for-er-well, for colds,' I finished lamely.
\par
'I see your kettle's boiling,' she said. 'Can I have some hot water?'
\par
'Hot water? Certainly.' I was a little puzzled, but I did not want to antagonize Miss Bellows at our first meeting.
\par
'Thank you. And a glass.'
\par
She took a glass and I went to get the kettle, From the pocket of her voluminous dress, she extracted two small packets, similar to those containing chemists' powders. Opening both packets, she poured first a purple powder and then a crimson powder into the glass. Nothing happened.
\par
'Now the water, please,' she said.
\par
'It's boiling hot!'
\par
'Never mind.'
\par
I poured boiling water into her glass, and there was a terrific fizzing and bubbling as the frothy stuff rose to the rim. It gave off a horrible stench. The potion was so hot that I thought it would crack the glass, but before this could happen, Miss Bellows put it to her lips and drained the contents.
\par
'I think I'll be going now,' she said, putting the glass down and smacking her lips. The cat, tail in the air, voiced its agreement. Said Miss Bellows, 'I'm much obliged to you, young man.'
\par
'Don't mention it,' I said humbly. 'Always at your service.'
\par
She gave me her thin body hand and held mine in an icy grip.
\par
I saw Miss Bellows and the black cat to the gate and returned pensively to my sitting room. Living alone was beginning to tell on my nerves and imagination. I made a half-hearted attempt to laugh at my fancies, but the laugh stuck in my throat. I couldn't help noticing that the broom was missing from its corner.
\par
I dashed out of the cottage and looked up and down the path. There was no one to be seen. In the gathering darkness, I could hear Miss Bellows' laughter, followed by a snatch of song:
\vspace{0.4in}
\par
\textit{With the darkness round me growing,}
\par
\textit{And the moon behind my hat,}
\par
\textit{You will soon have trouble knowing}
\par
\textit{Which is witch and witch's cat.}
\vspace{0.4in}
\par
Something whirred overhead like a Diwali rocket.
\par
I looked up and saw them silhouetted against the rising moon. Miss bellows and her cat were riding away on my broomstick.
\chapter{Grandfather Fights an Ostrich}
Before grandfather joined the Indian Railways, he worked for some time on the East African Railways, and it was during that period that he had his famous encounter with the ostrich. My childhood was frequently enlivened by this oft-told tale of my grandfather's, and I give it here in his own words-or as well as I can remember them:
\vspace{0.1in}
\par
While engaged in the laying of a new railway line, I had a miraculous escape from an awful death. I live in a small township, but my work lay some twelve miles away, and although I had a tent on the works, I often had to go into town on horseback.
On one occasion, an accident happening to my horse, I got a lift into town, hoping that someone might do me a similar favour on my way back. But this was not to be, and I made up my mind next morning to make the journey on foot, shortening the distance by talking a cut through the hills, which would save me about six miles.
\par
To take this shortcut, it was necessary to cross an ostrich 'camp' or farm. To venture across these 'camps' in the breeding season, especially on foot, can be dangerous, for during this time the male birds are extremely ferocious.
\par
But being familiar with the ways of ostriches, I knew my dog would scare away any ostrich that tried to attack me. Strange through it may seem, even the biggest ostrich (and some of them grow to a height of nine feet) will bolt faster than a racehorse at the same of even a small dog. And so, in company with my dog (a mongrel who had adopted me the previous month), I felt reasonably safe.
\par
On arrival at the 'camp', I got through the wire fencing and, keeping a good lookout, dodged across the spaces between the thorn bushes, now and then getting a sight of the birds that were feeding some distance away.
\par
I had gone about half a mile from the fencing when up started a hare, and in an instant my dog gave chase. I tried to call him back although I knew it was useless, since chasing hares was a passion with him.
\par
Whether it was the dog's bark on my own shouting, I don't know, but just what I was more anxious to avoid immediately happened: the ostriches were startled and began darting to and fro. Suddenly, I saw a big male bird emerge from a thicket about a hundred yards away. He stood still and stared at me for a few moments; then, expanding his wings and with his tail erect, he came bounding towards me.
\par
Believing discretion to be the better part of valour (at least in that particular situation), I turned and ran towards the fence. But it was an unequal race. What were my steps of two or three feet against the creature's great strides of sixteen to twenty feet? There was only one hope: to wait for the ostrich behind some bush and try to dodge it till it tried. A dodging game was obviously my only chance.
\par
Altering course a little, I rushed for the nearest clump of bushes where, gasping for breath, I waited for my pursuer. The great bird was almost immediately upon me, and a strange encounter commenced. This way and that, I dodged, talking great care that I did not get directly in front of its deadly kick. The ostrich kicks forward, and with such terrific force that his great chisel-like nails, if they stuck, would rip one open from head to foot.
\par
Breathless, and really quite helpless, I prayed wildly for help as I circled the bush, which was about twelve feet in diameter and some six feet in height. My strength was rapidly failing, and I realized it would be impossible to keep up the struggle much longer; I was ready to drop from sheer exhaustion. As if aware of my condition, the infuriated bird at me. With a desperate effort, I managed to step to one side. How it happened I don't know, but I found myself holding on to one of the creature's wings, close to its body.
\par
It was now the bird's turn to be frightened, and it began to turn, or rather waltz, moving round and round so quickly that my feet were soon swinging out almost horizontally. All the time the ostrich kept opening and shutting its break with loud snaps.
\par
Imagine my situation as I clung desperately to the wing of the enraged bird, which is whirling me round and round as if I had been cork! My arms soon began to ache with the strain, and the swift and continuous circling was making me dizzy. But I knew that if I relaxed my hold, a terrible fate awaited me: I should be promptly trampled to death by the spiteful bird.
\par
Round and round we went in a great circle. It seemed as if my enemy would never tire. But I knew I could not hold on much longer.
\par
Suddenly, the bird went into reverse! This unexpected movement not only had the effect of making me lose my hold but sent me sprawling to the ground. I landed in a heap at the foot of the thorn bush. In an instant, almost before I had time to realize what had happened, the ostrich was upon me. I thought the end had come. Instinctively, I put up my hands to protect my face. But, to my amazement, the great bird did not strike.
\par
I moved my hands from my face, and there stood the ostrich with one foot raised, ready to rip me open! I couldn't move. Was the bird going to play with me like a cat with a mouse, and prolong the agony?
\par
As I watched fascinated, I saw it turn its head sharply to the left. A second later, it jumped back, turned and made off as fast as it could go. Dazed, I wondered what had happened.
\par
I soon found out, for, to my great joy, I heard the bark of my truant dog, and the next moment he was jumping around me, licking my face and hands.
\par
Needless to say, I returned his caresses most affectionately! And I took good care to see that he did not leave my side until we were well clear of the ostrich 'camp'.
\chapter{A Bicycle Ride with Uncle Ken}
Kissing the girl while sharing the bicycle with her is no easy task, but I managed it when I was thirteen and my cousin Melanie was fourteen. Of course, we both fell off in the process, and landed in one of Granny's flower beds where we were well cushioned by her nasturtiums.
\par
I was a clumsy boy, always falling off bicycles, and Cousin Melanie was teaching me to ride properly, making me sit on the front seat while she guided the infernal machine from the carrier seat. The kiss was purely expermental. I had not kissed a girl before, and as Cousin Melanie seemed eminently kissable, I thought I'd start with her. I waited until we were stationary, and she was instructing me on the intricacies of the cycle chain, and then I gave her a hurried kiss on the cheek.
\par
She was so startled that she fell backwards, talking me and the bicycle with her.
\par
Later, she reported me to Granny, who said, 'We'll have to keep an eye on that boy. He's showing signs fo a dissolute nature.'
\par
'What's dissolute, Uncle Ken?' I asked my favourite uncle.
'It means you're going to the dogs. You're not supposed to kiss your cousin.'
\par
'Can I kiss other girls?'
\par
'Only if they are willing.'
\par
'Did you ever kiss a girl, Uncle Ken?'
\par
Uncle Ken blushed. 'Er . . . well . . . a long time ago.'
\par
'Tell me about it.'
\par
'Another time.'
\par
'Now tell me now. How old were you?'
\par
'About twenty.'
\par
'And how old was she?'
\par
'A bit younger.'
\par
'And what happened?'
\par
'We went cycling together. I was living in Agra, when your grandfather worked there with the railways. Daisy's father was an engine driver. But she didn't like engines-they left her covered in soot. Everyone had a bicycle in those days, only the very rich had cars. And the cars could not keep up with the bicycles.
\par
'We lived in the cantonment, where the roads were straight and wide. Daisy and I went on cycle rides to Fatehpur Sikri and Secunderabad and, of course, the Taj Mahal, and one evening we saw the Taj Mahal by moonlight and it made us very romantic, and when I saw her home, we kissed under the Ashoka trees.'
'I didn't know you were so romantic, Uncle Ken. Why didn't you marry Daisy?'
\par
'I didn't have a job. She said she'd wait until I got one, but after two years, she got tired of waiting. She married a ticket inspector.'
\par
'Such a sad story', I said. 'And you still don't have a job.'
\par
Uncle Ken had been through various jobs-private tutor, salesman, shop assistant, hotel manager (until he brought about the closure of the hotel) and cricket coach, this last on the strength of bearing a close resemblance to Bruce Hallam-but at present he was unemployed, and only too ready to put his vast experience of life at my disposal.
\par
Not only did he teach me to ride a bicycle but also accompanied me on cycle rides around Dehra and along the lanes and country roads outside the town.
\par
A bicycle provides its rider with a great amount of freedom. A car will take you further, but the fact that you're sitting in a confined space detracts from the freedom of the open spaces and unfamiliar roads. On a cycle, you can feel the breeze on your face, small the mango trees in blossom, slow down and gaze at the buffaloes wading in their ponds, or just stop anywhere and get down and enjoy a cup of tea or a glass of sugar cane juice. Footslogging takes time and cars are too fast-everything whizzes past you before you can take a second look-and car drivers hate having to stop; they are intent only on reaching their destinations in good time. But a bicycle is just right for someone who likes to take a leisurely look at the world as well as to give the world a chance to look at him.
\par
Uncle Ken and I had some exhilarating bicycle rides during my winter holidays, and the most memorable of these was our unplanned visit to a certain 'Rest Home' situated on the outskirts of the town. It isn't there now, so don't go looking for it.
\par
We had cycled quite far that day, and were tired and thirsty. There was no sign of a tea shop on that particular road, but when we arrived at the open gate of an impressive building with a signboard saying 'Rest and Recuperation Centre', we presumed it was a hotel or hostelry fo sorts and rode straight into the premises. There was an extensive lawn to one side, surrounded by neat hedges and flowering shrubs. A number of people were strolling about on the lawn; some were sitting on benches; one or two were straddling a wall, talking to themselves; another was standing alone, singing to a non-existent audience. Some were Europeans; a few were Indians.
\par
We left our cycles on the porch and went in search of refreshment. A lady in a white sari gave us cool water from a surahi and told us we could wait on a bench just outside their office. But Uncle Ken said we'd prefer to meet some of the guests and led me across the lawn to where the singer was practising his notes. He was a florid gentleman, heavily built.
\par
'Do you like my singing?' he asked, as we came up.
\par
'Wonderful!' exclaimed Uncle Ken. 'You sing like Caruso.'
\par
'I am Caruso!' affirmed the tenor and let rip the opening notes of a famous operatic aria. 'Your tiny hand is frozen,' he sang, although it was an unusually warm day.
\par
We hurried on a met an elegant couple who were parading up and down the lawn, waving their hands to an invisible crowd.
\par
'Good day to you, gentlemen,' said a flamboyant individual. 'You're the ambassadors from Sweden, I suppose.'
\par
'If you so wish,' said Uncle Ken gallantly. 'And I have the honour of speaking to-?'
\par
'The Emperor Napoleon, of course.'
\par
'Of course. And this must be the Empress Joséphine.' Uncle Ken bowed to the lady beside him.
\par
'Actually, it's Marie Walewska,' said Napoleon. 'Joséphine is indisposed today.'
\par
I was beginning to feel like Alice at the Mad Hatter's tea party, and began tugging at Uncle Ken's coat sleeve, whispering that we were getting late for lunch.
\par
A turbaned warrior with a tremendous moustache loomed in front of us. 'I'm Prithviraj Chauhan,' he announced. 'And I invite you to dinner at my palace.'
\par
'Everyone's royalty here,' observed Uncle Ken. 'It's such a privilege to be with you.'
\par
'Me too,' I put in nervously.
\par
'Come with me, boy, and I'll introduce you to the others.' Prithviraj Chauhan took me by the hand and began guiding me across the lawn. 'There are many famous men and women here. That's Marco Polo over there. He's just back from China. And if you don't care for Caruso's singing, there's Tansen, under that tamarind tree. Tamarind leaves are good for the voice, you know that, of course. And that fashinable gentleman there, he's Lord Curzon, who used to be a viceroy. He's talking to the Sultan of Marrakesh. Come along, I'll introduce you to them . . . You're the young prince fo Denmark, aren't you?'
\par
'Hamlet himself,' said Uncle Ken with a wink.
\par
Before I could refute any claims to royalty, we were intercepted by a white-coated gentleman accompanied by a white-coated assistant. They looked as through they were in charge.
\par¨
'And what are you doing here, young man?' asked the senior of the two.
\par
'I'm with my uncle,' I said, gesturing towards Uncle Ken, who approached and gave the incharge an affable handshake.
\par
'And you must be Dr Freud,' said Uncle Ken. 'I must say this is a jolly of a sort of place.'
\par
'Actually, I'm Dr Goel. You must be the new patient we were expecting. But they should have sent you over with someone  a little older than this boy. Never mind, come along to the office and we'll have you admitted.'
\par
Uncle Ken and I both protested that we were not potential patients but had entered the grounds by mistake. We had our bicycles to prove it! But Dr Goel was having nothing of this deception. He and his assistant linked arms with Uncle Ken and marched him off to the office, while I trailed behind, wondering if I should get on my bicycle and rush back to the Granny with the terrible news that Uncle Ken had been incarcerated in a lunatic asylum.
\par
Just then, an ambulance arrived with the real patient, a school principal suffering from a persecution complex. He kept shouting that he was perfectly sane, and that his entire staff had plotted to have him put away. This might well have been true, as the staff was there in force to make sure he did not escape.
\par
Dr Goel apologized to Uncle Ken. Uncle Ken apologized to Dr Goel. The good doctor even accompanied us to the gate. He shook hands with Uncle Ken and said, 'I have a feeling we'll see you here again.' He looked hard at my uncle and added, 'I think I've seen you before, sir. What did you say your name was?'
\par
'Bruce Hallam,' said Uncle Ken mischievously, and rode away before they charged their minds and kept him in their 'Rest Home'.
